# The Cost of Investment

Every instrument used to fund a startup -- cloud credits, venture equity,
a corporate strategic investment -- carries the same asymmetry,
regardless of its specific terms. The investor is running a portfolio:
many bets, most expected to fail, one or two needed to return the whole
fund. This is a statistical method. In contrast, the entrepreneur is
running a single bet. Egotistical, in the literal sense of putting
themselves first, because there is no alternative.

Three instruments carry this asymmetry in different currencies: cloud
credits, venture equity, and legal-fee-locked investment. Each looks,
from the outside, like support.

## Cloud credits

Startup credit programmes from AWS, GCP, and Azure seem pure upside:
free infrastructure, which is often believed to be the expensive part of
development. The term is short, usually a year or two, and the credits
are denominated in that provider's own services. The architecture is
then aligned around that provider's specific primitives.

When the credits run out, two costs land at once: the infrastructure
bill, now at full price, and the cost of migrating away from whatever
fit the credit programme.

At Bay Information Systems we took AWS credits for
[Marigold's](https://marigold.run) early infrastructure. Even after
advising people not to do this, we accepted, believing we could manage
the risk ourselves as dedicated technical professionals. The grant term
was the usual year or two. We burned through the entire thing in two
days -- a genuine burst of pace, immediately followed by a wait for
next-stage approval that moved on nobody's clock but the provider's.
The redevelopment away from the credit trough was a cost we paid
entirely ourselves. Free infrastructure with an expiry date is a loan,
priced in architecture.

## Venture equity

A venture fund needs one company in ten, or one in twenty, to return the
whole fund several times over. The other nineteen can fail without
threatening the fund's economics. This is the stated model rather than
a hidden one. And for egotistical reasons every founder thinks "I will
be the success." The terms are shaped to protect the fund's outcome:
dilution across successive rounds, board seats, liquidation preferences,
pressure toward growth that produces a fundable next round rather than
a sustainable business.

Three specific consequences of the same model:

A follow-on round depends partly on merit and partly on signalling. If
the existing investors decline to reinvest, that decision is visible to
every new investor evaluating the round, and reads as a verdict on the
company.

Paying contractors and early staff in equity rather than cash produces
a noisy cap table: a growing list of small stakeholders who were never
party to the company's later decisions, and who become creditors of
unclear priority if the company fails -- a question usually only worked
out during the wind-down itself.

Secondary market risk. An investor's stake is also their asset to sell.
A fund raising its own next round, or simply rebalancing, can sell your
shares on a secondary market. The new holder inherits any board seats
and information rights, likely without retaining the working
relationship that came with the original investor.

## Legal-fee-locked investment

The least discussed. An investment in a smaller company can appear to
solve many problems, but can also come with caveats: warranties,
information rights, consent requirements over specific decisions,
rights of first refusal on funding rounds. Drafting and reviewing the
agreement can run to real money in legal fees, before a single pound of
the investment has done anything operationally.

The company no longer has free rein. Despite the influx of cash, the
new paper changes what it is allowed to decide alone.

## The common structure

Five different instruments, one underlying pattern. The investor's
position is diversified across many bets and survives isolated
failures. The entrepreneur's position is not diversified at all; the
single bet is the whole position, staked on the one company they put
first. What changes between the instruments is only the currency of the
asymmetry: compute, dilution, signalling, cap table complexity, or legal fees.

What a round values the company at tells you almost nothing about who is carrying the risk. The terms attached to that valuation do. Worth checking before signing anything, cloud credits included.
